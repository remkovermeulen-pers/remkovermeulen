Good Product Strategy,
Bad Product Strategy.

Applying Richard Rumelt's principles to navigate the complexities of modern product development — and why most roadmaps fail before the first sprint begins.

01

The Diagnosis Problem

Most product strategies fail not because the plan is wrong, but because there was never a real diagnosis to begin with. Teams jump from ambition to roadmap, skipping the uncomfortable work of describing the actual obstacle in front of them. The result is motion without movement — quarter after quarter of velocity charts that hide a stalled business.

Richard Rumelt's framing is useful precisely because it refuses to be inspirational. A good strategy, he argues, is honest about what is hard, focused on what is decisive, and ruthless about what to ignore. It looks less like a vision deck and more like a piece of forensic analysis.

A strategy that fits on a slide is rarely a strategy. It's a wish, padded with verbs. — Working note, 2024

In practice, the diagnosis is the hardest artefact to produce because it forces the organisation to admit constraints it would rather euphemise. The leader's job is to make that admission survivable.

02

The Kernel of Strategy

Rumelt's kernel has three parts, and they only work together. A diagnosis names the challenge. A guiding policy describes the approach to that challenge. A coherent set of actions translates the policy into decisions that compound. Drop any of the three and the remaining two start to drift.

Diagnosis

A theory of the situation. What is actually getting in the way of progress, in concrete terms?

Guiding Policy

The approach you'll take in response — broad enough to direct, specific enough to forbid alternatives.

Coherent actions are where most teams discover that their strategy was an aspiration. When the trade-offs land on a real backlog, the seams show: two squads pulling toward incompatible policies, a flagship feature that violates the diagnosis, a metric chosen for comfort rather than truth.

03

Recognising Bad Strategy

Bad strategy has a vocabulary. Watch for fluff disguised as insight — "leverage AI to unlock customer-centric outcomes" — and for goals stated as if they were plans: "increase retention by 20%." A goal is not a strategy; it is the thing a strategy is supposed to produce.

The second tell is the failure to confront. A document that lists ambitions without naming the principal obstacle is usually political cover, not direction.

The third is mistaking templates for thought. A weighted impact-effort matrix can clean up a list, but it cannot decide what belongs on the list in the first place.

04

Putting it into Practice

A useful exercise: write the diagnosis on a single page, in plain language, with no framework borrowed from a consulting deck. Read it aloud to a stranger in your industry. If it doesn't make them say something more specific than "interesting," it's not done.

The guiding policy should be sharp enough to make at least one stakeholder unhappy. If everyone in the room nods along, you've written a values statement, not a policy.

Finally, list the coherent actions and look for one that you would defend in a downturn. That's the one to fund first. Everything else is optionality.
